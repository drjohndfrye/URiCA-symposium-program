Historical Institutional Barriers in the American Educational System: Disproportionate Burnout Among Teachers of Color in K-12 Schools

Poster Location 11

Cyrano Rivera, History Education major

Mentor: Tony Gulig, History

Abstract: All teachers face burnout in their profession, but teachers of color often face a disproportionate level of burnout. The main source of this burnout does not primarily stem from the actions of individuals; rather, it stems from historical institutional barriers that shape the American educational system. The term historical institutional barriers is defined in this study as past actions that the United States took to segregate and discriminate against teachers of color in the educational system. The methodology collects data on the experiences of all teachers in K-12 schools in the United States regardless of their race or ethnicity, asking if they experienced burnout in the profession. If they experienced burnout, this study then asks if their racial or ethnic identities accentuated the level of burnout they experienced. This aims to gather the perspectives of the participants on these questions through a racial and ethnic lens by asking open and closed-ended questions in a mixed-methods methodology. The anticipated findings are that participants who identify as teachers of color will tie their experiences of burnout to an adverse modern political climate, which the literature review asserts as being constructed from historical institutional barriers. Existing research does not study historical institutional barriers as a potential cause of disproportionate burnout due to constructing an adverse modern political climate. This study aims to explain the effects of historical institutional barriers in the American educational system by detailing how they cause teachers of color to face disproportionate burnout by creating an adverse modern political climate.
